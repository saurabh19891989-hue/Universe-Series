# 21 — Ferdowsi, Shahnama: Zal and the Simurgh (Persia)

- **Author / tradition:** Abolqasem Ferdowsi (Tus, Khorasan).
- **Original language and date:** Persian verse, completed c. 1010 CE.
- **Edition actually read:** Arthur George Warner and Edmond Warner, *The Shahnama of Firdausi*, vol. I (London: Kegan Paul, 1905; Trübner's Oriental Series), verse translation — Internet Archive (University of Toronto copy).
- **URL opened:** https://archive.org/download/shahnama01firduoft/shahnama01firduoft_djvu.txt
- **Rights:** US public domain (1905). The Warners' death dates are not verified here — check before quoting at length outside the US.
- **What I read:** The reign of Minuchihr: Sam's dream and his search for the son he abandoned; the Simurgh's speech to Zal and the gift of the feather. Background on Zal's birth (white hair) by context only.

## Story mechanisms
1. **The child rejected for his appearance, raised by a bird.** The hero Sam abandons his son Zal on Mount Alburz because the baby was born white-haired. The Simurgh takes him to her nest and raises him.
2. **The parent shamed by dream.** Sam dreams a rider calls him "impious": if a bird is nurse enough, what use is it being a paladin? If white hair is a blemish, look at your own beard.
3. **Named for the wrong done to him.** The Simurgh named the boy Dastan, which Warner glosses "much defrauded", "because thy sire dealt with thee treacherously". (Compare Odysseus, named for his grandfather's anger: card 15.)
4. **The foster-parent sends the child back, against her own heart.** She tells Zal the court will make her nest look small, and that she sends him away not in enmity but "to a kingship".
5. **The feather to burn.** She gives him one feather: if he is ever in trouble, burn it and she will come. *A summoning token used once, at the right moment.*

## Images and objects
- A nest on a peak "midst the Pleiades", built of ebony and sandalwood posts laced with aloe-wood, made without hands.
- A white-haired youth walking about a giant bird's nest, speaking in the bird's own accents.
- A single feather given as protection.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- The Simurgh: maternal, wise, unsentimental, grieving silently ("mine is breaking").
- Zal: grateful, reluctant to leave the nest for a throne.
- Sam: proud, guilty, roaring in his sleep.
- Quotation (Warner): "Then burn the feather and behold my might."

## What retellings get wrong
- The Simurgh is often reduced to a magic bird; in the text she is a reasoning foster-mother who chooses to give the boy back.

## Sensitivities
- The passage is full of prayer to God and Zoroastrian/Islamic framing; keep religious language out of comic scenes.
- Persian culture: avoid "Arabian Nights" conflation; the Shahnama is Iran's national epic.
