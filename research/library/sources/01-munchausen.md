## Verification note (important)
The cannonball ride **is not in the English Raspe text** I read (the 1895 reprint, which follows the 5th edition plus the 1793 additions), and a text search of the 1786 *Gulliver Revived* and the 1889 English edition also found only cannon duels and caught shells, not the ride. It appears in the Bürger-derived German text. So the most famous Munchausen image is a German addition to an English book about a real German nobleman.

## Story mechanisms
1. **The delayed truth.** The post-horn will not sound in the frost; that evening, hung by the kitchen fire, it plays all the tunes by itself as they thaw. *A story told in the cold comes true later, somewhere warm.* Our use: the Baron's lies have not stopped coming true; they are frozen and will "thaw" at the wrong moment.
2. **The abandoned ride.** In the German text the Baron leaps onto a cannonball to spy on the besieged town. Halfway through the air he has second thoughts: he will get in easily, but how will he get out, and won't they hang him as a spy? So he jumps across to a ball flying the other way and comes home *having achieved nothing*. Mechanism: the hero turns back mid-air; courage is not the point, the swap is.
3. **The rope that is cut from above to lengthen below.** Coming down from the Moon on a straw rope, he cuts the useless top and ties it to the bottom, again and again, until it snaps four or five miles up. A perfect engine for a scheme that eats itself.
4. **The broken horse that goes on drinking.** The rear half of his horse was cut off by a portcullis; the front half drinks and the water runs straight out. Sewn up with laurel shoots, the horse grows a bower of laurel over the rider. Mechanism: an injury turns into a decoration.
5. **Cannonball meets cannonball.** At Gibraltar he times his gun so the two balls collide in mid-air and the enemy's flies back. Use for any duel of matched timing.
6. **The dare to the doubter.** Chapter endings dare the listener: whoever doubts may leave before the next part. The Baron's authority rests on the audience staying in the room.
7. **Crossover.** The *Sequel* has Don Quixote charge the Baron's procession, taking the Sphinx and Gog and Magog for enemies. Precedent, in a 1792 text, for our legends meeting.

## Images and objects
- A silver hatchet, badge of the Sultan's gardeners, found on the Moon "in a heap of chaff and chopped straw" where everything shines like silver.
- A Turkey-bean stalk hooked onto one of the Moon's horns; then a rope of lunar straw.
- A post-horn on a peg by the fire playing "The King of Prussia's March" to an empty kitchen.
- The second Moon: a ship carried up by a hurricane; Lunarians 36 feet tall who unscrew their heads and send them out alone to spy; eye-dealers selling spare eyes; hailstones that are grape-pips from Moon vineyards.
- A half-horse under a laurel bower.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- He narrates to a table of gentlemen, flatters them as fellow sportsmen, and threatens doubters politely. He never laughs at his own story.
- He prefaces wonders with modesty ("modesty forbids") and then claims the whole victory.
- He is practical in absurdity: every impossible fix is described as ordinary workmanship, with measurements.
- Quotation (from the 1895 text, ch. XVIII): "let him take a voyage there himself, and then he will know I am a traveller of veracity."

## What popular retellings get wrong
- The cannonball ride is not Raspe's, and in the source it is a *retreat*, not a triumph: he turns back halfway.
- The famous Moon voyages are two different trips, one comic-agricultural (beanstalk, straw rope), one a ship blown into the sky; neither uses a cannon or balloon.
- Most of the book was not even by Raspe: the publisher's continuators wrote the later chapters "in the Baron's manner". The Baron can be annoyed that he is a franchise.

## Sensitivities
- Ch. VI begins with the Baron enslaved by "the Turks" and other chapters use period caricature of Turks and Africans. Do not reproduce.
- The real Baron Hieronymus von Münchhausen (1720–1797) was a living person who resented the book; our Baron is the book's character, not the man.
