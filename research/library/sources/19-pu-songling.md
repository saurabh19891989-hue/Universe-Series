# 19 — Pu Songling, Strange Stories from a Chinese Studio (Liaozhai zhiyi)

- **Author / tradition:** Pu Songling (Qing China).
- **Original language and date:** Classical Chinese, compiled c. 1679–1707, printed 1766.
- **Edition actually read:** Herbert A. Giles, *Strange Stories from a Chinese Studio*, vol. I (London: De La Rue, 1880) — Internet Archive (University of Toronto copy).
- **URL opened:** https://archive.org/download/strangestoriesfr00pusuuoft/strangestoriesfr00pusuuoft_djvu.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain everywhere (Giles d. 1935).
- **What I read:** I "Examination for the Post of Guardian Angel"; V "The Taoist Priest of Lao-shan"; the table of contents.

## Story mechanisms
1. **The afterlife civil-service exam.** A scholar is summoned by an official messenger with a white-faced horse to sit an exam. The theme is eight words; his answer (virtue by intention is not rewarded, wickedness without intention not punished) wins him the post of a city's guardian deity. He weeps: his old mother has no one else.
2. **The furlough negotiated from the Book of Fate.** The presiding officials have the Book of Fate brought, find his mother has nine years to live, appoint another candidate as a stand-in, and grant him a **nine-year furlough**. He wakes in his coffin three days dead. Bureaucracy that is humane because it checks the register.
3. **The paper moon.** A Taoist master cuts a circle of paper, sticks it on a wall, and it becomes a moon bright enough to see a hair by. A guest throws a chopstick into it and the moon-lady Chang'e steps out, dances and sings that her home in the moon is too lonely and cold, then becomes a chopstick again. The guests carry their table *into* the moon and drink there.
4. **The bottomless kettle.** One kettle of wine serves every disciple and never empties.
5. **The power that fails when you brag.** The would-be disciple, after months of wood-chopping, begs for one small trick; he learns to walk through walls if he rushes at them head down. Warned to use it carefully, he goes home, boasts to his wife, rushes the wall — and gets a bump the size of an egg.

## Images and objects
- A white-faced horse sent to fetch a sick man to an exam.
- The Book of Fate fetched by a long-bearded clerk.
- A circle of paper on a wall becoming the moon.
- A chopstick becoming a dancing woman a foot high who grows to full size.
- A man on the floor with an egg-sized bump, his wife laughing.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- The examiners: kindly, precise, and procedural; they solve a human problem by finding the right entry.
- The Taoist master: amused, testing patience with labour, generous but dry.
- Mr Wang: impatient, entitled, a braggart punished by his own trick.
- Quotation (Giles, Chang'e's song): "Too lonely and cold is my home in the moon."

## What retellings get wrong
- Giles, like many Victorians, renders the city god as a "Guardian Angel", Christianising it; and footnotes Chang'e as the wife who stole the elixir and fled to the moon — itself one version among several.

## Sensitivities
- Taoist practice and city gods are living traditions; keep priests and gods off-screen as comic figures. The mechanisms (exam, furlough, paper moon, bragging) transfer to secular officials.
- Chang'e is also a contemporary Chinese space-programme name; avoid any confusion with real missions.

## Episodes
Ep 4 (the humane furlough from the register; an exam to leave), ep 2 (the paper moon, Chang'e as Kaguya's neighbour; the lonely, cold Moon), ep 8 (the trick that fails when you brag — Wukong's jailers).
