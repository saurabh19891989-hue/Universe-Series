# 14 — Jules Verne: Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea and The Mysterious Island (Nemo)

- **Author / tradition:** Jules Verne (France).
- **Original language and date:** French; *Vingt mille lieues sous les mers* 1869–70; *L'Île mystérieuse* 1874–75.
- **Editions actually read:** (a) *Twenty Thousand Leagues under the Sea*, Project Gutenberg #164 (an early English translation; the translator is not named in the file). (b) *The Mysterious Island*, Project Gutenberg #1268 (translator not named in the file; it is the text usually identified as W. H. G. Kingston's 1875 version — attribution not verified here). (c) For comparison, the French original *L'Île mystérieuse* (1875), Project Gutenberg #14287.

- **What I read:** *Twenty Thousand Leagues* Part I ch. X "The Man of the Seas" (first conversation), the library/salon description, and Part II ch. XXI ("A Hecatomb": "I am the law"); *Mysterious Island* Part III, Nemo's life story as Prince Dakkar, in English, and the same passage in French.

## Key finding: the English translation changed Nemo's story
In the French, Dakkar is the son of a rajah of Bundelkhand and **nephew of Tipu Sultan**; his father sent him to Europe "so that he might one day fight on equal terms with those he considered his country's oppressors"; he becomes "the soul" of the 1857 uprising, fighting beside "heroes who had risen to free their country"; a price is put on his head, and **his father, mother, wife and children paid for him** before he knew they were in danger; "right, once again, had fallen before might."

The English text in Gutenberg #1268 removes Tipu Sultan, says his father hoped to raise his "long degraded and heathen country to a level with the nations of Europe", calls the uprising an "atrocious mutiny" by "misguided fanatics" persuaded by "designing chiefs", praises the British for bringing "peace and prosperity" — and **omits the killing of his family**, which is the reason for his hatred. Earth did not just get Nemo wrong; a translator rewrote his motive out of the book. (Canon already says: Nemo is Verne's Indian prince, anti-colonial, played seriously. This is why.)

## Story mechanisms
1. **Identity by separate testimony.** Nemo pretends not to understand his prisoners' languages and lets each tell his story. "The story told by each one, entirely agreeing in the main points, convinced me of your identity." An interrogation disguised as rudeness. (Ideal for ep 9: he already knows things he lets others reveal.)
2. **The right to forget you exist.** He tells Aronnax he could put them back on deck, dive, "and forget that you had ever existed. Would not that be my right?" (Delisting as a personal act.)
3. **The wreck as a bank.** Nemo funds independence movements from the galleons sunk at Vigo Bay in 1702 — he lives off what the empires lost.
4. **The man who broke all ties.** He calls himself a man who "has broken all the ties of humanity"; his name, Nemo, is Latin for "no one". (Nobody meets Nobody in ep 9.)
5. **Judge and wronged party at once.** Before sinking a warship: "I am the law, and I am the judge! I am the oppressed, and there is the oppressor!" — the victim who makes himself the court.
6. **The confession at the end.** He tells his whole life only when dying, to strangers he has secretly protected for years.

## Images and objects
- A library and salon under the sea with paintings and a piano-organ, pearls in cases "larger than a pigeon's egg".
- A coral cemetery where his crew are buried, "a quiet grave".
- Gold from sunken galleons funding distant revolts.
- The Nautilus's motto *Mobilis in mobili* ("moving in the moving").
- (In *Leagues*, after the sinking) a portrait of a woman and two children before which he weeps — the same family the English *Mysterious Island* forgot to mention.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- Exquisitely courteous, multilingual, and cold; hospitality as a form of control.
- Scientific enthusiasm breaks through his reserve, then he falls silent mid-sentence.
- Generous anonymously (to a pearl-diver, to rebels), merciless openly.
- Quotation (Gutenberg #164): "I am the law, and I am the judge! I am the oppressed, and there is the oppressor!"

## What retellings get wrong
- He is usually shown as a European eccentric with a beard. Verne's final word is that he is Prince Dakkar, an Indian anti-colonial fighter whose whole family was killed.
- The English-speaking world read a censored motive for a century.

## Sensitivities
- 1857 remains contested history in India and Britain; per canon play it seriously. Avoid "maharaja" orientalist costume; Dakkar is a European-educated scientist and prince.
- Nemo's violence (sinking warships with crews) must cost something on screen.
