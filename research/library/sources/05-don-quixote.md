# 05 — Don Quixote

- **Author / tradition:** Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra (Spain).
- **Original language and date:** Spanish, Part I 1605, Part II 1615.
- **Edition actually read:** John Ormsby's translation (1885), Project Gutenberg #996.
- **URL opened:** https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/9/9/996/996-0.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain everywhere (Ormsby d. 1895).
- **What I read:** I.8 (windmills and the Biscayan, including the manuscript breaking off mid-blow); search-checked I.9 (Cide Hamete Benengeli); II.10 (Sancho "enchants" Dulcinea); II.35 and II.71 by search (Merlin's 3,300 lashes; Sancho paid per lash, whipping trees); II.64 (the Knight of the White Moon); II.72 (Don Álvaro Tarfe's sworn declaration); II.74 (sanity, will and death).

## Story mechanisms
1. **The madman's explanation absorbs every failure.** After the windmill, Quixote says the enchanter who stole his books turned the giants into mills to rob him of glory. Nothing can disprove him.
2. **The squire's lie inverts the madness.** In II.10 Sancho, who never found Dulcinea, points at three plain village girls on donkeys and swears she is a princess on white palfreys. Quixote sees *only peasant girls* — he is the one seeing truly — and concludes an enchanter has transformed her. The sane man produces the delusion; the madman is right about the facts.
3. **Cure by defeat.** The Knight of the White Moon (the bachelor Samson Carrasco in disguise) defeats Quixote on Barcelona beach and binds him by his own code: go home and lay down arms for a year. A kindly fraud uses the madman's honour as the lever.
4. **Sanity kills.** Home, defeated, he sleeps six hours, wakes as "Alonso Quixano the Good", renounces chivalry, makes his will and dies. His friends think the sanity is a *new* madness. (Our ep 3 premise, already in Cervantes.)
5. **Sancho offers to take the blame to restart the story.** At the deathbed Sancho begs him to get up and go shepherding: say you lost because "I girthed Rocinante badly". The squire offers to falsify the record so the knight can keep living.
6. **The legal affidavit of authenticity.** In II.72 Quixote meets Don Álvaro Tarfe, a character from the *spurious* sequel by Avellaneda, and has him swear before the mayor and a notary that this is not the Don Quixote of that book. A character from a fake version certifies the real one.
7. **Disenchantment paid by the stroke.** Merlin decrees 3,300 lashes on Sancho's backside to free Dulcinea; Quixote ends up paying Sancho per lash, and Sancho whips the beech trees in the dark while groaning.
8. **The story breaks off mid-blow.** I.8 ends with swords raised because the manuscript runs out; the narrator finds the rest in Arabic in a Toledo market. A cliffhanger caused by a missing document.

## Images and objects
- Thirty or forty windmills whose sails begin to turn in a sudden breeze just as he charges; the lance shattered, horse and rider rolled across the plain.
- A shining moon painted on an enemy's shield.
- Three girls on donkeys; one prods her mount with a spiked stick and gallops off while the knight kneels.
- Sancho thrashing beech trees at night, counting.
- A will that leaves the housekeeper her back wages plus twenty ducats for a dress, and forbids the niece to marry any man who reads chivalry books.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- Quixote: courteous and formal even when beaten; always "commends himself to Dulcinea" before a fight; corrects others' facts with gentle firmness ("not saying you lie, but that you are not correct").
- Sancho: proverbs by the fistful; cowardly in words, loyal in deeds; a natural haggler (he negotiates his own whipping fee).
- Quixote at the end is lucid, sorry, and generous — he frees Sancho from all debts.
- Quotation (II.74): "in last year's nests there are no birds this year."

## What retellings get wrong
- The windmills take about a page in Chapter 8 of a two-part, 126-chapter novel. They are not the centre of the book.
- Quixote is not simply fooled by everyone: in Part II people stage fake adventures *for* him (the Duke and Duchess, Carrasco), and he often sees ordinary reality more clearly than they do.
- He is furious about the unauthorised sequel; Cervantes makes the real Quixote fight a fake one. (He has *already* been annoyed at how Earth told his story — since 1615.)

## Sensitivities
- Spanish-language culture: avoid flamenco-and-bull cliché; La Mancha's real textures (dust, mills, inns, barbers, priests) are better.
- Part II includes the expulsion of the Moriscos (Ana Félix's story, II.63–65). If used at all, treat seriously.
