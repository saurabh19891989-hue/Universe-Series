# 03 — Orlando Furioso, cantos 34–35 (Astolfo on the Moon)

- **Author / tradition:** Ludovico Ariosto (Ferrara, Italian Renaissance chivalric romance).
- **Original language and date:** Italian verse, 1516 (final edition 1532).
- **Edition actually read:** William Stewart Rose's verse translation (1823–31), Project Gutenberg #615.
- **URL opened:** https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/6/1/615/615-0.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain everywhere (Rose d. 1843).
- **What I read:** Canto 34, stanzas LXVIII–XCII (the chariot of Elijah, arrival on the Moon, the Valley of Lost Things, the flasks of wits, the palace of the Fates); Canto 35, stanzas I–XXX (the old man who throws name-plates into Lethe, the swans, the temple of Immortality, and St John's speech on poets).

## The inventory of the Valley (as the text lists it)
"Whatever on our earth below we lose ... lost through time, chance, or our own folly." Specifically:
- fame eaten by time like moths; countless vows and prayers;
- lovers' tears and sighs; time wasted at play; the idleness of ignorant men; vain plans; empty desires (which fill most of the valley);
- a hill of swollen bladders that shout — the crowns of old Assyrian, Lydian, Greek and Persian kingdoms, now nameless;
- gold and silver hooks — gifts courtiers give to buy favour; snares hidden in garlands — flattery;
- burst cicadas — the praise-poems of paid poets;
- gold and jewelled bands — loves that ended badly;
- eagles' talons — authority great lords lend their deputies;
- bellows — the favour of kings' favourites;
- overturned towns and castles — treaties and failed conspiracies;
- serpents with women's faces — coiners and robbers;
- broken bottles — service at wretched courts;
- a spilled pool of porridge — charity left in wills;
- a heap of rotten flowers — the Donation of Constantine;
- twigs and birdlime — women's charms (Ariosto's joke, not ours);
- one's own lost days and deeds, unrecognisable without the guide;
- and a mountain of **sense** (wits): a thin liquid kept in corked flasks of every size, each labelled with its owner's name. The biggest says ORLANDO'S WIT.
- **Not there:** madness. It never leaves the Earth.

## Story mechanisms
1. **Lost things have an address.** Everything lost goes somewhere specific and is stored. (The Valley as a lunar lost-property office in ep 2.)
2. **The labelled flask.** Wits are liquid, corked, labelled with names. Astolfo finds his own flask too — he had lost wits without knowing — and sniffs it back up his nose. Then, says the narrator, he later lost them again.
3. **People are surprised by whose wits are there.** Many supposedly wise men have flasks far fuller than he expected — they have lost most of theirs.
4. **Names thrown into the river.** In the palace of the Fates, every life is a thread, and each finished life has its name on a metal plate. An old man (Time) carries armfuls of plates to the river Lethe and dumps them. Crows and vultures snatch names but drop them; only two white swans carry a few names to a hill where a nymph hangs them on a pillar in the temple of Immortality. **This is an Atlas origin myth ready-made:** names fall out of memory unless someone carries them to an index.
5. **Fame is paid for.** St John (Astolfo's guide) says the poets made heroes great because heirs paid them. He goes further: read Homer "by contraries" — the Greeks ran, Troy won, and Penelope was unfaithful.
6. **The cure is a reversal of the journey.** Astolfo takes Orlando's flask home to make a madman sane. (Mirror of our ep 3, where we must make a sane man mad.)

## Images and objects
- The Moon seen close: as large as Earth, its seas and rivers different from ours, and the Earth from it a little hoop you must strain to see.
- Elijah's fiery chariot drawn by four horses redder than flame.
- A hill of bladders that still shout the names of dead empires.
- Flasks of wit, corked, labelled, in rows.
- Swans carrying metal name-plates out of a river of forgetting, past crows who drop theirs.

## Character behaviours (Astolfo, in our words)
- Curious and practical: he asks what each heap means rather than marvelling.
- He takes his own wits back without hesitation, then forgets the lesson.
- He travels by borrowed transport (the hippogriff, then Elijah's chariot) and never seems to own anything.
- Quotation (Rose, 34.LXXXI): madness is the one infirmity not found there, "Which dwells below, nor leaves this earthly ball."

## What retellings get wrong
- The Valley is usually shown as a cosy attic of lost socks. In Ariosto it is a satire on courts: bribes, flattery, paid praise, broken treaties.
- The guide is not a wizard but the apostle John, and his point is political: legends are what patrons pay for.
- Penelope's fidelity is mocked in this very passage — fuel for our running joke and a real threat to Jax's story (see 15-odyssey).

## Sensitivities
- The guide is St John the Apostle, a revered figure of a living religion. Do not put him on screen as a comic character; give his role to an original keeper (Kaguya, per canon) or to Astolfo himself.
- Ariosto's lines on "women's witcheries" are period misogyny; drop them.

## Episodes
Ep 2 (the Valley; the jar labelled *Nobody*), ep 4 and ep 10 (name-plates and the swans as an Atlas myth; Oyuna as a "swan"), ep 10 (Voss as the patron who pays poets: "read the legend by contraries"), ep 3 (a flask of wits as a prop).
