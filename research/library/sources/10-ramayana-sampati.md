# 10 — Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda: Sampati's story (cantos LVI–LXIII)

- **Author / tradition:** Valmiki (Sanskrit epic, India).
- **Original language and date:** Sanskrit verse; composed over several centuries BCE–early CE.
- **Edition actually read:** Ralph T. H. Griffith, *The Rámáyan of Válmíki, translated into English verse* (1870–74), one-volume text — Project Gutenberg #24869, with Griffith's notes.
- **URL opened:** https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/2/4/8/6/24869/24869-0.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain everywhere (Griffith d. 1906). M. N. Dutt's prose version not consulted.
- **What I read:** Book IV, cantos LVI (Sampáti) to LXIII (Sampáti's Story), complete, and the opening of LXIV. Griffith's numbering matches the brief's 58–63 for the core of the story (the flight is told in LVIII and LXI).

## The story in the text (our summary)
The monkey search-party, having failed their deadline, sit down on a mountain to fast to death. A huge old vulture, Sampati, sees them and is glad of a meal. When they mention his brother Jatayu's death, he cries out: he has not heard his brother's name in years. He cannot fly — "the sun has burnt my wings". Long ago, in youthful pride, the two brothers vowed before the sages to chase the sun to where it sets. As they climbed, the heat overcame Jatayu; Sampati spread his own wings over his brother to shield him and lost every feather, falling onto the Vindhya mountain. He lay unconscious seven nights, found a hermit he once visited, and wanted to throw himself off the cliff. The sage told him to wait: one day messengers would come, and when he told them what he knew, his wings would grow back. He waited a hundred seasons, fed by his son, who once came back without food because he had watched a demon carry a woman south. Now, having told the searchers where she is (vultures can see a hundred leagues), Sampati's new wings sprout in front of them and he flies.

## Comparing the two sun-flights (with 09-ovid)
| | Icarus (Ovid VIII) | Sampati (Ramayana IV) |
|---|---|---|
| Who flies | A boy with artificial wings made by his father | Two adult bird-brothers, kings of vultures, with their own wings |
| Why | Escape from prison; then joy carries him higher | A boastful vow before sages to race the sun to its setting |
| Who warned | The father, explicitly (keep the middle) | Nobody; pride is acknowledged afterwards |
| What fails | Wax melts; the boy falls and drowns | The younger brother faints; the elder shields him and burns his own wings |
| Who survives | The father (guilty of an older murder) | The elder brother (the sacrificer); the younger also survives but they are separated for life |
| After | Burial; the land named after the boy | A century of grounded waiting, shame and hunger |
| Redemption | None; the partridge rejoices | Wings regrow the moment he tells what he has seen |
| Moral shape | Grief and hidden guilt | Shame turned into usefulness by bearing witness |

The deep difference: Icarus's story ends at the fall; Sampati's *begins* there. One is about flying too high; the other is about what you do for a hundred years after.

## Story mechanisms
1. **The shield that burns the shielder.** Spreading your wings over another means losing them yourself.
2. **A prophecy that sets a waiting job.** He must stay put and wait for strangers; the reward is conditional on telling them the truth.
3. **Witness as cure.** Healing comes not from rest or medicine but from passing on information.
4. **Hunger mistaken for menace.** His first words are about eating the searchers; they suspect a trap. The fierce old creature is actually the ally.
5. **The far-seer who cannot move.** Supreme sight, no mobility: he can see the target a hundred leagues away but must send others.
6. **Stacked air.** Sampati lists the layers of the sky by which birds fly there (pigeons lowest, then crows, kites, falcons, vultures, swans, and Garuda's line highest) — a ready-made altitude map.

## Images and objects
- A featherless giant bird on a mountain ledge above a group of starving searchers.
- Cities below "like chariot wheels", rivers like silver thread, and the sun swelling till it looks as big as the Earth.
- Young pinions bursting from an old body in front of witnesses.
- A son returning to his father with no food, only a story.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- Sampati: proud, ashamed of his pride, ferocious in hunger and tender about his brother; he asks first for news, then offers what he knows.
- He speaks with the authority of age and the lore of the sky.
- He asks to be carried to the sea to make offerings for his brother — he finishes mourning before he flies.
- Quotation (Griffith, LVI): "The sun has burnt my wings, and I / No longer have the power to fly."

## What retellings get wrong
- Popular retellings compress him into "the vulture who told Hanuman where Sita was". In the text he is the hero of his own tragedy — a sacrifice, a hundred-season penance, and a resurrection.
- He is not a monster; the searchers briefly think Death (Yama) himself has come for them, but Sampati is a grieving king.

## Sensitivities
- Per canon: Sampati is heroic and dignified; no Rama, Sita or Hanuman as characters. In our version the information he gives must be about our own story (e.g. a person or ship he saw from far away), not about Sita.
- The Ramayana is scripture to many Hindus. Treat Sampati as an epic figure with respect; no jokes at his burning or his hunger.
