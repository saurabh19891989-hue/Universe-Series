# 11 — Anansi (Kwaku Ananse) stories, Akan tradition

- **Author / tradition:** Akan peoples (Asante, Fante and others), present-day Ghana; told as *Anansesem*, "spider stories".
- **Original language and date:** Twi and Fante oral tradition; collected in the colonial period.
- **Editions actually read:** (a) R. S. Rattray, *Akan-Ashanti Folk-Tales*, collected and translated, Oxford: Clarendon Press, **first published 1930** (the Archive scan is a later lithographic reprint of the same text), bilingual Twi/English, illustrated by Asante, Fante and Ewe artists. (b) W. H. Barker and Cecilia Sinclair, *West African Folk-Tales*, London: Harrap, 1917.
- **URLs opened:** https://archive.org/download/akanashantifolkt0000ratt/akanashantifolkt0000ratt_djvu.txt · https://archive.org/download/westafricanfolkt00barkrich/westafricanfolkt00barkrich_djvu.txt
- **Rights:** Rattray (d. 1938): UK/EU/India public domain; US public domain since 1 Jan 2026 (1930 publication). Barker (d. 1929) and Sinclair: 1917, US public domain; Sinclair's dates not verified. The stories themselves are traditional; credit the Akan origin (per canon).
- **What I read:** Rattray's introduction (on the opening and closing formulas and the name "Anansesem"), tale 17 "How it came about that the Sky-god's stories came to be known as 'Spider-stories'" in full (English and parallel Twi). Barker & Sinclair tales I "How we got the name 'Spider tales'", II "How wisdom became the property of the human race", and III "Anansi and Nothing", all in full.

## Tale 1: how Ananse bought the Sky-god's stories (Rattray 17)
Great towns have tried to buy the stories and failed. Ananse, "a mere masterless man", asks the price: the python, the leopard, a fairy (*mmoatia*) and the hornets. He offers, unasked, to add his own old mother. **His wife Aso devises each trap.** The python: Ananse argues aloud with his "wife" whether a palm branch is longer than the python; the python offers to be measured and is tied to it. The hornets: he sprinkles water over them and himself, holds a plantain leaf over his head, and invites them into his gourd out of the "rain". The leopard: a covered pit, then a mock-friendly rescue with two sticks, then the knife. The fairy: a carved doll smeared with sticky latex, with mashed yam in her hands; when the fairy slaps the "rude" doll that does not answer her thanks, she sticks fast. Each delivery, the Sky-god says "My hand has touched it; what remains still remains." At the end he calls his elders, lists the price received, and gives the stories to Ananse forever.

## Tale 2: the pot of wisdom (Barker II)
Anansi, offended, collects all the wisdom in the world into one pot to hide it at the top of a tree. He ties it in front of him and cannot climb. His son suggests tying it on his back. Realising the boy has wisdom he lacks, Anansi throws the pot down; it breaks and wisdom spreads over the world.

## Tale 3: Anansi and Nothing (Barker III)
A rich neighbour called **Nothing** and poor Anansi go to find wives. Anansi persuades Nothing to swap clothes "for a little while", delays giving them back, and wins many wives in Nothing's velvet; Nothing in rags wins only one. At the crossroads Nothing's servants are waiting with carpets; his wife becomes queen; Anansi's wives leave him. Anansi kills Nothing with a trap of knives and slippery steps. The widow pays children with mashed yam to cry with her — which is why a crying child is said to be "crying for nothing".

## Story mechanisms
1. **The impossible price paid in instalments, with receipts.** "My hand has touched it; what remains still remains" — a ledger formula. (Our con can be run as a payment schedule.)
2. **The staged argument overheard.** Ananse quarrels with an imaginary opponent so that the victim volunteers to settle it (python, and in Barker the bees). Flattery by dispute.
3. **The fake weather.** He creates the conditions (rain) that make the trap look like shelter.
4. **The sticky effigy.** The victim is caught by her own sense of manners: she slaps the doll for not saying thank you.
5. **The bonus nobody asked for.** He throws in his own mother. The con artist upsells himself into debt.
6. **The real planner is uncredited.** Aso plans every capture; the stories are named after him.
7. **Borrowed identity becomes stolen identity.** Anansi wears Nothing's clothes and takes his place; the clothes, not the man, win the wives.
8. **Hoarding destroys the hoard.** The pot of wisdom breaks precisely because he tries to own it.

## Images and objects

- A man drenched and holding a plantain leaf over his head on a sunny day.

- A pot of wisdom smashed on a rock.
- A widow carrying a dish of yam round the district, paying children to cry for Nothing.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- Ananse brags upward ("Rather, I shall be able") and haggles sideways; he insults his catches ("Fool!") as soon as they are tied.
- He consults his wife at every step and takes the credit.
- He is petty and vengeful when he loses (the Nothing story is genuinely dark).
- Quotation (Rattray's closing formula, English): "if it be sweet, (or) if it be not sweet, take some elsewhere, and let some come back to me."

## Two formulas worth using
- Opening (Rattray): "We do not really mean, we do not really mean (that what we are going to say is true)."
- Closing: the sweet/not-sweet formula above. A story is a loan that circulates.

## What retellings get wrong
- Most modern versions list three prices; Rattray's Asante version has four plus his mother, and his wife designs the traps.
- Barker's 1917 English version turns the leopard into a "tiger" — there are no tigers in West Africa; a translator's slip. (Anansi will be annoyed.)
- He is not a cuddly trickster; in "Nothing" he murders a rival.

## Sensitivities
- Per canon: credit the Akan origin; the Sky-god (Nyame / Nyankonpon) stays off-screen. Rattray notes stories about the Sky-god are told *as* not-true by formula; we keep that distance.
- Avoid generic "African" set dressing; use Akan specifics (odum tree, Twi formulas, kente only with care, talking drum per canon music).
- Colonial-era collectors' framing (Rattray was a British officer) should not shape our tone.
