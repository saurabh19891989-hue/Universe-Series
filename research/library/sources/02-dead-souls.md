# 02 — Dead Souls

- **Author / tradition:** Nikolai Gogol (Russian Empire; Ukrainian-born).
- **Original language and date:** Russian, Part I 1842 (Part II fragments posthumous).
- **Edition actually read:** Translated by D. J. Hogarth, Everyman's Library (J. M. Dent), 1915, with John Cournos's introduction — Project Gutenberg #1081.
- **URL opened:** https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/1/0/8/1081/1081-0.txt
- **Rights:** US public domain (published 1915). Gogol d. 1852. Hogarth's death date not verified — check before quoting at length in UK/EU; our paraphrase is unaffected. Hapgood's 1886 translation (also PD) not consulted.
- **What I read:** ch. I (Chichikov's arrival and manner), ch. II (the pitch to Manilov), ch. III (Korobochka), ch. V (Sobakevich), and ch. XI (Chichikov's backstory as a customs official and the smuggling scheme); skimmed ch. IV (Nozdryov) and ch. VI (Plyushkin) by search.

## How Chichikov pitches (the core of this card)
- **Manilov (sentimental):** he glances over his shoulder before asking "how long since your last census return?", gets the bailiff to admit nobody counts the dead, then asks for a written list. Only then says he wants the dead peasants *who are still listed as alive*. When Manilov is stunned, he reassures him with legality: he never steps outside the civil law; on paper they will be written as living.
- **Korobochka (thrifty widow):** pure tax argument — you keep paying poll tax on the dead, I take the burden off you and pay you fifteen roubles each. She fears being cheated precisely because it has never been done; he compares the dead to rags (which at least sell for paper) and to her honey (which cost labour; these cost nothing). He sweats, curses her under his breath, and changes route.
- **Sobakevich (heavy, grasping):** Chichikov opens with a long speech about the greatness of the Empire and its statutes, and calls the souls "non-existent", never "dead". Sobakevich instantly says "you are after dead souls?" as if discussing grain, asks a hundred roubles a head, and sells the *talents* of the dead (the wheelwright, the carpenter three arshins tall) as if they could still work.
- The pattern: **he adapts his vocabulary to the seller**, prefers euphemism, uses the state's own paperwork as the product, and lets the seller name the moral problem first.

## Story mechanisms
1. **Buying what the register says exists.** The value is entirely in the list, not the people. (Our use: Voss buys legends that are off the Atlas but still on old paper.)
2. **Relief, not purchase.** The pitch is framed as taking a burden off the seller (the tax on the dead). Voss's acquisitions can be framed as "relieving" a port of a legend it can no longer afford to list.
3. **The euphemism ladder.** Dead → non-existent → "souls on the census list" → "they exist only in idea". Each seller forces a different word.
4. **The seller who over-believes.** Sobakevich praises the skills of the dead men he is selling. A seller who sincerely haggles over the worth of nothing is funnier than one who doubts.
5. **The incorruptible official as the best smuggler.** In ch. XI Chichikov becomes a ferociously honest customs officer, waits until he controls the whole search system, then tells the smugglers "the time is NOW". Zeal is the cover.
6. **The famous fraud nobody recounts.** The narrator refuses to retell the "oft-repeated" Spanish-sheep smuggling story (lace carried between double fleeces), assuming we know it. A legend referenced but never shown.

## Images and objects
- Chichikov himself: neither handsome nor ugly, neither fat nor thin, neither old nor young — and his arrival causes no stir. Two peasants discuss only his carriage.
- Manilov's pipe gurgling like a bassoon while he tries to understand.
- Sheep crossing a frontier in double fleeces with Brabant lace between the layers.
- The customs officer's courteous phrases while searching every button and the inside of horses' ears.
- French soap that whitens the skin, which he keeps asking for.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- Polite to the point of invisibility; remembers every name and rank; changes manner to match each host.
- Never says the ugly word; makes the other person say it.
- Rages privately, smiles publicly; wipes his brow and tries another road.
- Quotation (ch. II, Hogarth): "Never do I permit myself to step outside the civil law."

## What retellings get wrong
- He is usually played as a sinister mastermind. In the text he is forgettable, nervous, sweating, and keeps nearly losing deals to stubborn old women.
- His real past is customs and anti-smuggling work, not crime — he was the state's best officer before he was its best cheat. (Directly useful for Mira, who works a customs desk in ep 1.)

## Sensitivities
- Ch. XI contains antisemitic remarks about Jewish smugglers. Not to be echoed.
- Serfdom is the real system being satirised; the trade in people should stay clearly horrible even when the scenes are funny. Our Chichikov trades in *listings* of legends, but the audience should feel the human cost.
