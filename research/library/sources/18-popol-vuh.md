# 18 — Popol Vuh (K'iche' Maya): the Hero Twins in Xibalba

- **Author / tradition:** K'iche' Maya sacred narrative (highland Guatemala), written in the 16th c. in Latin script; copied by Francisco Ximénez c. 1701; first published in Europe by Brasseur de Bourbourg (1861).
- **Original language and date:** K'iche', mid-16th c. (older tradition).
- **Edition actually read:** Lewis Spence, *The Popol Vuh: The Mythic and Heroic Sagas of the Kichés of Central America* (London: David Nutt, 1908; "Popular Studies in Mythology" series). This is a **summary and commentary in English**, based mainly on Brasseur's French, not a full translation.
- **URL opened:** https://archive.org/download/popolvuhmythica00spengoog/popolvuhmythica00spengoog_djvu.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain in the US (1908) and, since Spence died in 1955, in UK/EU as of 2026. The Popol Vuh is sacred to K'iche' communities today.
- **What I read:** Spence's summary of Books I–III, focusing on Book II (the first twins' defeat, the second twins' ordeals, their death and return, the sun and moon), and his notes on Xibalba.

## Story mechanisms
1. **The message delivered by nested couriers.** The lords of Xibalba summon the twins. Their grandmother sends a louse; to go faster the louse lets a toad swallow it, the toad a snake, the snake a great bird. At the end each frees the one inside — but the louse never really got swallowed: it hid in the toad's gums. The message arrives anyway. (A courier gag for the crew's first delivery.)
2. **The cane in the house.** Before leaving, each twin plants a cane in the middle of the house that will wither if he dies. Grandmother watches the canes green and wither.
3. **The mosquito that pricks the court.** On the way, they send a creature ahead to prick every lord with a hair from Hunahpu's leg. The real lords cry out and name each other; the ones that don't are **wooden figures** set up to trick visitors into bowing. The twins do not bow to the mannequins.
4. **Houses of ordeal.** A series of "houses" — Gloom, Lances, Cold, Tigers (jaguars), Fire, Bats — each a test with a trick solution (bribing the lancers, warming with pine-cones, allies among the ants). In the House of Bats a head is cut off; Spence notes the text is unclear how it is restored.
5. **Die on purpose, return as entertainers.** The twins tell two seers how to dispose of their bones, burn themselves, are ground and thrown in the river, and come back first as fish-men, then as ragged old vagabonds performing miracles: burning houses and restoring them, killing and reviving each other.
6. **The con the lords ask for.** The lords of Xibalba ask to see the trick done on themselves — to be killed and revived. The twins kill them and **do not revive them**. Then they announce their real names and set the terms of the lords' diminished future.
7. **Becoming sun and moon.** After honouring their dead father and uncle, those two (or in other readings the twins) rise to become the sun and moon; the 400 youths become stars.

## Images and objects
- A louse inside a toad inside a snake inside a bird.
- Two canes in the centre of a house, greening and withering.
- Wooden lords on thrones, and a bench that is a red-hot stone.
- A rubber ball and the ball-court as the place of challenge.
- Two ragged old dancers burning a palace and setting it back up.

## Character behaviours (the Twins, in our words)
- Patient, forewarned and polite; they learn from their fathers' mistakes.
- They win by allies (ants, animals) and by research before arrival.
- They perform humility (as beggars) and reveal their names only at the moment of victory.
- No quotation (Spence summarises rather than translates).

## What retellings get wrong
- The twins do not merely survive the underworld; they defeat it by staging a con that the lords request.
- Spence himself warns that his base text (Brasseur's) is unclear at key points; most English summaries hide those gaps.

## Sensitivities
- Living sacred text of K'iche' Maya people; treat the Twins as heroes, keep Xibalba's lords as an original analogue (our lords are a Voss board, not Maya deities), and avoid "Aztec/Maya temple" set clichés and sacrifice imagery.
- Use a modern K'iche'-informed translation for any detail before production; Spence is a Victorian-era populariser.
