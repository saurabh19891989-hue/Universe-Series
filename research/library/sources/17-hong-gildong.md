# 17 — Hong Gildong (Hong Kil Tong), Korea

- **Author / tradition:** *Hong Gildong jeon*, Korean prose tale traditionally attributed to Heo Gyun (1569–1618); circulated in many vernacular versions.
- **Original language and date:** Korean (Hangul), 17th c. or later in surviving forms.
- **Edition actually read:** Horace Newton Allen, *Korean Tales: being a collection of stories translated from the Korean folk lore*, New York and London: Putnam, 1889, tale IX "Hong Kil Tong; or, The Adventures of an Abused Boy" — Internet Archive (Harvard copy via Google).
- **URL opened:** https://archive.org/download/koreantalesbein00allegoog/koreantalesbein00allegoog_djvu.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain everywhere (Allen d. 1932). Allen's version is a free 1889 retelling and differs from the standard Korean text (e.g. Hong's island kingdom, Yuldo, appears only as "an island"); use it for mechanisms, check a modern scholarly summary before claiming fidelity.
- **What I read:** The whole tale (about 20 pages).

## Story mechanisms
1. **The son who may not say "father".** Born to a concubine, Kil Tong is placed with the slaves and forbidden to call his father "father"; the law that makes him nobody is the engine of the whole story.
2. **The household plots, the father wavers.** The wife and another concubine bribe a fortune-teller to predict he will kill his family; the father locks him up and later sends assassins, then falls ill and calls them off, then sends them again.
3. **The room that becomes a mountain.** When the assassins burst in, the room vanishes; they are on a mountainside in a storm; a boy rides past on a donkey playing a flute — Kil Tong.
4. **Join the outlaws by passing their exam — your own way.** The robbers test newcomers; he chooses his own test and throws a boulder.
5. **The fake noble son with a fake delivery.** He robs a rich temple by arriving as a nobleman's son sent to study, announcing a hundred ponies of rice as a gift; robbers ride in the rice sacks. At the feast he "finds" a pebble in his rice and makes the hosts bow in shame — then they are tied up.
6. **Eight of him, each with the scar.** After raids reported in all eight provinces on the same day, each governor sends a captured Kil Tong. Eight identical men stand in line. The father says his son has a scar on the left thigh; all eight pull up their trousers and show the scar. Ordered killed, they turn out to be figures of **straw and wax**. *Identification by body mark defeated by copies.*
7. **Demand the rank you were denied.** He posts a letter on the palace gate: give me my father's rank and the raids stop. The court agrees, planning to kill him when he bows; he is carried off in a cloud. Later he lands from a stork in the moonlit royal garden, takes the rank, borrows three thousand bags of rice and leaves for an island — and later **repays the rice**.

## Images and objects
- A crow cawing three times at a window.
- A boy on a donkey playing the flute on a stormy mountain.
- A stone door in a cliff opening on a hidden valley of two hundred houses.
- Robbers inside rice sacks on a hundred ponies.
- Eight identical men in a line, each lifting his trouser leg to show the same scar.
- A man descending into a moonlit palace garden on a stork, playing a flute.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- Proud, wounded, principled: he robs officials and corrupt institutions, not the poor.
- He spares those who tried to kill him, on condition they never kill again.
- He wants recognition (rank, the word "father") more than money; he settles his debts.
- Quotation (Allen): "a scar on the left thigh. Instantly each of the eight men pulled up the baggy trousers and displayed a scar."

## What retellings get wrong
- His fame as Korea's "John Doe" (the sample name on forms) has nothing in the tale; the tale is about a man whose name the law would not recognise. (Per canon, Seo carries his name on cover papers; the real Hong Gildong arrives in S2 to ask who has been using it.)
- Allen's English presents him as a magical bandit; the Korean classic is read as a social protest against the status of illegitimate sons.

## Sensitivities
- Allen's version portrays the Buddhist temple's monks as corrupt and an island people as "savage" and "hairy"; avoid both. Korean Buddhism is a living religion.
- Per canon: one core character per country. Hong Gildong is a guest (S2), not crew.
