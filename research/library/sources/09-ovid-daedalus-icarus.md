# 09 — Ovid, Metamorphoses VIII.183–259: Daedalus and Icarus (with Perdix)

- **Author / tradition:** Ovid (Publius Ovidius Naso), Rome; Greek myth.
- **Original language and date:** Latin verse, c. 8 CE.
- **Edition actually read:** Henry Thomas Riley's literal prose translation, *The Metamorphoses of Ovid*, vol. II (Bohn, 1851), with Riley's notes — Project Gutenberg #26073.
- **URL opened:** https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/2/6/0/7/26073/26073-0.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain everywhere (Riley d. 1878).
- **What I read:** Book VIII, Fable III in full (lines 183–259) and Riley's "Explanation" after it.

## Story mechanisms
1. **The prisoner of land and sea escapes by the one realm the king doesn't own.** Minos holds land and sea; "he does not sway the air." Escape route as legal loophole.
2. **The middle course.** Daedalus warns: too low and the sea's damp clogs the wings, too high and the sun scorches them. Fly between. (Two failure modes, not one.)
3. **The child undoes the work while playing.** Icarus catches at the feathers in the breeze and softens the wax with his thumb, slowing the job.
4. **Joy, not defiance.** Icarus begins to enjoy "a bolder flight", leaves his guide, "touched with a desire of reaching heaven". He calls his father's name as he hits the water.
5. **The father's hidden crime.** As Daedalus buries his son, a partridge watches from a tree and chirps with delight. It is Perdix, his nephew and pupil, inventor of the saw and the compass, whom Daedalus threw from Athena's citadel out of jealousy, claiming it was an accident. Athena turned him into a bird. *The grieving inventor is a murderer, and his victim watches him bury his son.* Riley's note adds that Daedalus's exile was for this murder.
6. **The bird that fears heights.** Perdix as partridge never flies high or nests in trees; "mindful of its former fall, it dreads the higher regions." A flyer defined by fear of altitude.
7. **The rationalised version.** Riley's "Explanation" reports an ancient reading: there were no wings; Daedalus invented sails and outran Minos's oared galleys; Icarus fell overboard or died of fatigue. A myth "corrected" into engineering.

## Images and objects
- Feathers laid in order, shortest to longest, "as the rustic pipe rises by degrees with unequal straws"; bound with thread in the middle and wax at the base.
- Yellow wax softened under a boy's thumb.
- An angler, a shepherd and a ploughman looking up and taking the two flyers for gods.
- Wings floating on the water.
- A partridge on a holm-oak (Riley notes Ovid forgot partridges don't perch in trees).

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- Daedalus: brilliant, controlling, tender in the moment (cheeks wet, hands trembling as he fits the wings) and capable of murderous jealousy toward a gifted younger rival.
- Icarus: playful, distractible, delighted; not defiant.
- Perdix: the overlooked clever child who survives as something smaller.
- Quotation (Riley): "let Minos possess everything besides: he does not sway the air."

## What retellings get wrong
- The wings fail because Icarus forgets the middle course out of joy, and Daedalus *also* warned against flying too low.
- Daedalus is not a blameless grieving father; the same passage tells us he killed his nephew.
- Our canon has Icarus alive "who did it for joy" — the text supports the joy. Earth's moral ("pride comes before a fall") is a later gloss; Ovid's Icarus is simply happy.

## Sensitivities
- Low. Greek myth, no living cult. Avoid presenting the boy's death as a joke.
