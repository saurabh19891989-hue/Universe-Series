# 15 — Homer, The Odyssey (books 1, 9, 17, 19, 21, 23)

- **Author / tradition:** Homeric epic, Greek oral tradition.
- **Original language and date:** Ancient Greek hexameter, c. 8th–7th c. BCE.
- **Edition actually read:** Samuel Butler's prose translation (1900; Gutenberg text includes the 1922 second-edition preface) — Project Gutenberg #1727. Butler uses Latin names (Ulysses, Jove, Neptune, Minerva) and renders "Outis" as **"Noman"**.
- **URL opened:** https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/1/7/2/1727/1727-0.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain everywhere (Butler d. 1902).
- **What I read:** Book I (Calypso, daughter of Atlas); Book IX (the Cyclops: Noman, the blinding, the boast, the curse); Book XVII (Argos); Book XIX (Euryclea and the scar, with the naming by Autolycus); Book XXI (the bow's history, the suitors' failures, the stringing); Book XXIII (the bed test).

## This underlies the long secret: concrete, plantable details
**Name and address (Book IX)**
- The name trick works only because of grammar: Polyphemus shouts "Noman is killing me", and his neighbours hear "no man is killing me" and go back to bed.
- The curse is caused **not by "Noman" but by the boast**. Safely at sea, Ulysses cannot stay nobody: he shouts his full name, father and **address** — "Ulysses, son of Laertes, who lives in Ithaca". That gives the Cyclops something to curse.
- The curse is precise: may he reach home late, in sore plight, having lost all his men, **"in another man's ship"**, and find trouble in his house. *Plant: Jax flies a repossessed ship that is not legally his (ep 1 cold open).*
- The prophecy had told Polyphemus to expect a giant; he got "a little insignificant weakling". The legend did not look like his story.
- The blinding stake is **olive wood**. Olive marks both the first great lie and the last great proof.

**The scar (Book XIX)**
- A boar wound **above the knee**, received as a youth hunting on Mount Parnassus with his grandfather's sons. *Plant: Jax keeps one leg away from firelight / medical scanners; a scar he says is from a docking accident.*
- He turns away from the fire before the nurse washes him, because he knows she will recognise it. Recognition by touch, not sight: "I did not know you till I had actually touched and handled you."
- His grandfather **Autolycus**, "the most accomplished thief and perjurer in the whole world", named him: because he himself was angry with many people, call the boy the child of anger. *Plant: Jax's talent for lying is inherited; his very name was given by a con-man in a bad mood.*
- When the nurse recognises him, he grabs her by the throat and threatens her. The hero has a frightening edge.

**The dog (Book XVII)**
- Argos was bred by Ulysses but never worked for him; now neglected on the dung heap by the stable doors, full of fleas. He drops his ears and wags his tail but cannot get up. Ulysses wipes away a tear unseen and asks the swineherd about the "noble hound" as a stranger would. Argos dies as soon as he has recognised his master. *Plant: the Mars dog cannot be bribed, ignores everyone else, and is old; give it a fixed spot it returns to.*

**The bow (Book XXI)**
- The bow was a gift from Iphitus, met when the young Ulysses was sent to **recover a debt** (three hundred sheep stolen by the Messenians). He did not take it to Troy; he kept it at home as a keepsake. *Plant: Jax learned to shoot "collecting debts"; he refuses to carry a weapon he values.*
- The suitors warm and grease it and still cannot string it. He turns it over, checking whether worms have eaten the horns, and they mock him as "some tricky old bow-fancier". He strings it as easily as a bard fits a new string to a lyre; plucked, it **sings like a swallow**. *Plant: Jax handles any bow like an instrument and tests its string by ear.*
- The test is twelve axe-heads in a row; the arrow goes through them all.

**The bed (Book XXIII)**
- Penelope tests him with a lie: she orders the nurse to carry his bed out of the room. He is angry: no one could move it, unless someone has **cut the olive at its roots**. He built the room round a living olive, cut off its top, shaped the stump into a bedpost, bored it, inlaid it with gold and silver and stretched crimson leather across it. Only three people know: he, she, and one maid.
- *Implication for our parcel:* if a living piece of the olive bed is in the parcel, **someone has cut the tree**. The proof of his identity has been damaged — and the test of the marriage may already have been broken. Who cut it, and when?
- Penelope out-tricks the trickster. She was afraid of being fooled "with a lying story; for there are many very wicked people going about". *That is Voss's business model.*

**Calypso (Book I)**
- The goddess who keeps him on her island, trying "by every kind of blandishment to make him forget his home", is **the daughter of Atlas**, who carries the pillars that keep heaven and earth apart. Our index is called the Atlas. The one who erased Ulysses's way home is Atlas's daughter.

## Story mechanisms
1. A name that grammatically erases its bearer.
2. The boast that undoes the trick (the hero cannot bear anonymity).
3. A curse that specifies the route home.
4. Recognition by body mark and by touch.
5. Recognition by an animal that cannot be fooled.
6. A test only the owner can pass (strength + knack with an object).
7. A test of shared secret knowledge, administered by lying.

## Images and objects
Olive-wood stake hissing in an eye; a ram's belly with a man clinging under it; an old dog on a dung heap; a scar above the knee in firelight; a bronze basin of water knocked over as the nurse drops his foot; a bow that sings like a swallow; twelve axe-heads; a bedpost that is a living tree.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- He lies fluently and enjoys it, but breaks cover out of pride at the worst moment.
- He hides tears; grief is something he does not let witnesses see.
- He can be frighteningly ruthless with people who could expose him.
- Quotation (Butler, IX): "say it was the valiant warrior Ulysses, son of Laertes, who lives in Ithaca."

## What retellings get wrong
- The disaster is caused by the name-boast, not by the blinding alone.
- He is not simply noble: he threatens his old nurse with death.
- The bed is not a symbol; it is a technical object built round a living tree, and the test only works because it cannot move.
- (Ariosto, card 03, claims Homer lied about Penelope — the "by contraries" reading. Our Penelope can be sharper than any version.)
