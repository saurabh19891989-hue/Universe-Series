# 20 — Gessar Khan (Mongolian Geser epic), told by Ida Zeitlin

- **Author / tradition:** The Geser (Gesar) epic of Tibet and Mongolia, sung by bards across Central Asia; a Mongolian version was printed in Beijing in 1716 under the Kangxi emperor.
- **Original language and date:** Mongolian (1716 print), from much older oral tradition.
- **Edition actually read:** Ida Zeitlin, *Gessar Khan*, illustrated by Theodore Nadejen, New York: George H. Doran, 1927. Zeitlin's foreword says she retold it from Isaac Jakob Schmidt's German translation *Die Thaten Bogda Gesser Chan's* (St Petersburg, 1839), made from the 1716 text. **A literary retelling, not a translation.**
- **URL opened:** https://archive.org/download/gessarkhan0000unse/gessarkhan0000unse_djvu.txt
- **Rights:** US public domain (published 1927). Zeitlin's dates are not known to me — check before use outside the US. Schmidt's German (1839) is public domain everywhere and should be the base for any close use.
- **What I read:** The foreword; the chapter of the twelve-headed giant and "the drink of forgetfulness"; the opening of "The Three Shiraigol Khans"; the chapter of his awakening (the crone, the rook, the arrow-letter, the steed).

## Story mechanisms
1. **The victory drink that erases the victor.** After he kills the twelve-headed giant, the giant's wife Aralgo offers him a "cooling draught" in a golden chalice. It is the drink of forgetfulness; everything since his birth leaves his memory. He stays with her **nine years**.
2. **The catastrophe that happens while he forgets.** On that very day his enemies march, his brother vanishes, his thirty heroes are killed, his wife is captured.
3. **Evidence of time.** A crone's cow, a calf when he killed the giant, now has horns crumbling with age. He hears the time before he understands it.
4. **The bird that shames him.** A rook tells him it flies west to forage but goes home every night to guard its family — unlike him.
5. **The letter that arrives by arrow.** His wife's enchanted arrow falls into his empty quiver, with her message on the shaft. Reading it lifts the darkness of nine years. *A delivered message restores a man's identity.*
6. **The horse remembers when the hero doesn't.** His wonder-steed, chained in the dungeon, breaks free in rage, scolds him and runs off; he must ask his heavenly sisters to bring the horse back.

## Images and objects
- A black draught in a golden chalice.
- A cow with crumbling horns.
- An arrow landing in an empty quiver, words written on its shaft.
- A horse tearing off an iron halter and fetters.

## Character behaviours (Gessar, in our words)
- Invincible in battle, helpless against comfort and kindness.
- When he remembers, he weeps loudly and calls himself a fool; no self-justification.
- He depends on others who remember for him (wife, horse, sisters).
- Quotation (Zeitlin): "all that was befallen him since his birth fled from his memory."

## Why this matters for Oyuna
Geser is sung by bards in Mongolia and Tibet who learn very long epics by heart; it is the natural repertoire of a family of epic-singers. Its core episode is a hero who forgets himself for nine years and is restored by a message — exactly what Oyuna, the "only unedited copy", could do for Jax. Commission or consult a living Mongolian bard tradition before writing her performance; Zeitlin is a 1920s American retelling.

## What retellings get wrong
- Westerners tend to read Geser only as a warrior king; the Mongolian text spends a whole cycle on his forgetting and his failure to protect his people.

## Sensitivities
- Geser is venerated in Tibetan Buddhist and Mongolian folk religion and is a national epic for several peoples (Tibetan, Mongol, Buryat). Do not make him a comic guest; use the *structure* (forgetting, the arrow-letter) rather than the hero.
- Buddhist figures in the text (Buddha, Khormusta) must not appear as characters.
