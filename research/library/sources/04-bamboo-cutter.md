# 04 — The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter (Taketori monogatari)

- **Author / tradition:** Anonymous, Heian Japan; often called the oldest Japanese prose tale.
- **Original language and date:** Classical Japanese, late 9th or early 10th century.
- **Edition actually read:** F. Victor Dickins, *The Old Bamboo-Hewer's Story*, London: Trübner, 1888 (translation with romanised text and notes), Internet Archive scan.
- **URL opened:** https://archive.org/stream/oldbamboohewerss00dick/oldbamboohewerss00dick_djvu.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain everywhere (Dickins d. 1915).
- **What I read:** The setting of the five tasks; the Buddha's bowl, the jewelled branch of Hōrai, the fire-rat robe, and the dragon's jewel quests; the whole final section "The Celestial Robe of Feathers" to the burning on Fuji. Dickins **omits the fifth quest** (the swallow's cowry) from his main text, describing it only in a later note, which I did not read; I have not described that quest from this source.

## The five tasks (as Dickins gives them)
1. Prince Ishizukuri: the Buddha's stone begging-bowl from India.
2. Prince Kuramochi: a branch from the tree on Mount Hōrai with silver roots, gold trunk and white jade fruit.
3. The Minister Abe: a robe of the fur of the fire-proof rat from China.
4. The Dainagon Ōtomo: the rainbow jewel in a dragon's head.
5. The Lord Isonokami: the cowry shell a swallow brings over the sea (omitted by Dickins).

## Story mechanisms
1. **The impossible task as a lie detector.** She never says no; she asks for things that do not exist, and each suitor reveals himself in *how* he fakes or fails.
2. **The forgery exposed by unpaid workers.** Kuramochi has a perfect fake branch built in secret and tells a long sea-adventure lie. Mid-triumph, six craftsmen arrive with a petition: a thousand days of work, still unpaid. Kaguya laughs and sends him away. (Pure gold for ep 2: Lucía, still unpaid, exposes Jax's fake.)
3. **The test by fire.** The fur robe is thrown into the flames to prove it is fire-proof; it burns away in a flash. Proof destroys the evidence.
4. **The quest delegated and abandoned.** The Dainagon sends his men for the dragon's jewel with endless supplies; they split the supplies and go home. He waits a year in a redecorated house, then goes himself and is nearly killed by the storm the dragon raises.
5. **Each failure becomes a saying.** Every quest ends in a pun that, the text claims, gave Japanese a new idiom for shameless or fruitless effort. Failure enters the language.
6. **The siege that cannot win.** Two thousand guards on roof and ground, bows drawn; she is locked in a storehouse. She tells them every door will fly open. When the Moon people come in light ten times brighter than the full moon, the soldiers' strength drains and their arrows go astray. Doors open untouched.
7. **The robe that changes the heart.** "Who dons this robe changes his heart." She delays putting it on so she can finish writing; the moment it falls on her shoulders she forgets her foster-father's grief.
8. **The gift refused.** She leaves the elixir of life for the Emperor; he will not live forever without her and has it burned on the highest mountain. The smoke still rises: Fuji.

## Images and objects
- A tiny girl, the size of a rape-seed, glowing inside a bamboo stem.
- A forged jewelled branch delivered in a coffer, and craftsmen holding a petition in a split bamboo.
- A fur robe burning to nothing in a second.
- A storehouse door sliding open by itself in moonlight brighter than noon; soldiers who cannot lift their bows.
- A bamboo tube of elixir and a letter burned on a summit; smoke that never stops.

## Character behaviours (Kaguya, in our words)
- She sets conditions instead of refusing; polite, exact, and lethal.
- She enjoys exposure: her face "turned radiant with joy" when the forgery is revealed.
- She insists on finishing her letter before the robe takes her memory — she controls the last moment she will feel.
- Quotation (Dickins): "who dons yonder robe changes his heart, and I have still somewhat to say ere I depart."

## What retellings get wrong
- She is often shown as passive and sad. In the text she is a sharp, funny examiner who humiliates five powerful men and refuses an emperor.
- The Moon is not a paradise she longs for; she says she has forgotten her Moon parents and wants to stay. She was sent down to *expiate a fault* — a punishment, which the Moon envoy calls finished.
- Fuji's name: Dickins notes one spelling read as "not-dying" (immortal); the pun is about the elixir, not about soldiers.

## Sensitivities
- Japanese culture: avoid "kimono-and-cherry-blossom" generic exoticism; take the specific Heian details (bamboo, letters, poems, storehouse, palace guards).
- The Buddha's bowl is a sacred relic; keep it off-screen or as a named item only.
