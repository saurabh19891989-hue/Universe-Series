# 06 — Nasreddin Hodja (Nasr Eddin) tales

- **Author / tradition:** Anonymous joke-cycle about a village preacher-wit, told across Anatolia, Persia, the Arab world, the Balkans, the Caucasus and Central Asia (many countries claim him; per canon we assign no nationality).
- **Original language and date:** Turkish chapbook tradition (earliest manuscripts 16th c.; jokes older).
- **Edition actually read:** George Borrow, *The Turkish Jester; or, The Pleasantries of Cogia Nasr Eddin Efendi*, translated from the Turkish, Ipswich: W. Webber, 1884 (transcribed from the 1924 Norwich edition of Borrow's works) — Project Gutenberg #16244.
- **URL opened:** https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/1/6/2/4/16244/16244.txt
- **Rights:** Public domain everywhere (Borrow d. 1881).
- **Not found:** Henry D. Barnham, *Tales of Nasr-ed-Din Khoja* (1923) — Internet Archive search returned no copy. Not described here.
- **What I read:** The whole of Borrow's collection (about 120 short pleasantries).

## Ten tales whose logic can drive a bureaucratic farce (all in Borrow)
1. **The cauldron that gave birth and died.** He returns a borrowed cauldron with a small saucepan inside: "it gave birth." The owner keeps the saucepan. Next time he keeps the cauldron: "it died." *If you accepted the first absurd rule, you must accept its consequence.* → Phobos: if Phobos is exempt from every law, it is also exempt from the law that lets anyone hold you.
2. **"You believe the ass, not a grey-bearded man?"** He says the donkey is not home; it brays inside. *Testimony versus records.* → The form says Oyuna is not here; she is visibly here; whom do you believe?
3. **The wind blew me here.** Caught in a garden with a sack of vegetables: the wind blew him in, he grabbed plants to save himself; as for who filled the sack — "that is the very question I was about to ask myself." *Accept the premise, then interrogate the officer's next question as your own.*
4. **The ladder for sale.** Caught climbing a garden wall with a ladder: he is selling the ladder; "cannot a ladder be sold anywhere?" *Any act can be recategorised as permitted commerce.*
5. **"Eat, my pelisse."** Ignored at a feast in rags, honoured in a fur coat, he feeds the coat. *The status is in the uniform; address the uniform.* (PIP addressing the rubber stamp.)
6. **The pot of stones.** He counts the fast by dropping a stone a day into a pot; his daughter adds a handful; the pot says day 120, so he says it is the 45th to sound moderate. *A ledger corrupted by one outside input; the clerk rounds down to seem reasonable.*
7. **Playing dead, out for a walk.** Fleeing horsemen, he lies naked in a tomb: "I am one of the buried, but came here to walk." Elsewhere, in a coffin carried by scholars stuck in mud: "If I were alive I would get out of this place as quick as possible." *The dead have different rules.* → Filing Oyuna as deceased to get her out.
8. **The middle of the earth is under my donkey's hoof.** Three learned visitors ask unanswerable questions; he answers each with an unmeasurable claim and invites them to measure. *Unfalsifiable answer + burden of proof shifted to the questioner.* (Exactly how an exemption clause works.)
9. **The Cadi who tried to bite his own ear.** Judging whether a man bit his own ear, he goes away and tries; he cannot, falls, breaks his head, and rules: no man can bite his own ear, but he who tries may fall and break his head. *The official who tests the rule on himself.*
10. **The house with two doors.** He hides from guests; when they insist he came in, he leans out of the window: the house has two doors; he left by the other one. *Procedural alibi delivered in person.*

Also useful: "I neither buy nor sell the moon" (asked the moon's phase); "When there is a new moon, what becomes of the old one? They make forty stars out of each"; and the shepherd who kills travellers who cannot answer what happens to old moons — the Cogia says they make lightning of them. Phobos is slowly falling and will break into a ring: Nasreddin's old-moon jokes are literally on topic.

## Story mechanisms (summary)
1. Accept the absurd premise, then hold the other side to it.
2. Recategorise the act (theft → selling; trespass → wind).
3. Address the symbol of status instead of the person.
4. Shift the burden of proof onto the questioner.
5. Use the special rules of the dead, the absent, or the two-doored.

## Images and objects
- A cauldron with a baby saucepan in it.
- A fur pelisse being offered soup.
- A pot full of 120 stones.
- A man flat on his back beside a well, a snapped rope in his hands, delighted that the moon is "back in its place" in the sky.
- A tortoise tied to a spade: "you must now learn to work."

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- He never admits defeat; he reframes it as the intended outcome.
- He is cheerful and patient; the joke is always on the system, often on himself.
- He answers a question with a question that is slightly better.
- Quotation (Borrow): "I neither buy nor sell the moon."

## What retellings get wrong
- He is often a gentle sage only. In Borrow he also steals, lies, plays dead, and wins by shamelessness; the collection ends by admitting readers may find only "the ravings of madness" — or wisdom.
- Several famous modern Nasreddin jokes (e.g. paying for the smell of food with the sound of coins) are **not** in Borrow's collection; check other sources before attributing.

## Sensitivities
- Many tales are framed in Islamic practice (sermons, Ramadan, ablutions, mosque). Per canon: the comedy is logic, never religion. Do not use the sermon or prayer jokes.
- The thousand-gold-pieces tale casts a Jewish neighbour as the dupe and ends with him beaten; omit. Several tales are bawdy; Borrow censors some with dashes.
- One tale calls his disciple "an Abyssinian, black"; the joke is about skin colour; omit.
