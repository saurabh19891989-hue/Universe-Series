# 08 — The Thousand and One Nights: the frame story and Sindbad (voyages 1 and 7)

- **Author / tradition:** Anonymous Arabic story collection with Persian, Indian and Arab layers; Baghdad and Cairo redactions.
- **Original language and date:** Arabic; frame story attested from the 9th–10th c.; Sindbad cycle added later.
- **Editions actually read:** (a) Andrew Lang, *The Arabian Nights Entertainments* (Longmans, 1898; 1918 printing), following Galland's French — Project Gutenberg #128: frame story and all seven Sindbad voyages headings, voyages 1 and 7 read in full. ## Story mechanisms
1. **The confederate who asks on cue.** Scheherazade's plan depends on her younger sister (Dinarzade in Lang, Dunyazad in Burton) waking before dawn and asking for a story in agreed words. The technique is a two-person con, not a solo performance.
2. **Story as ransom, nested.** On the first night she tells of a merchant condemned by a jinni for killing his son with a date-stone; three old men buy a third of his life each with a story. The frame repeats inside itself.
3. **The warning tale that fails.** In Burton her father tries to dissuade her with a fable (the Bull and the Ass); she is not deterred. Stories do not automatically persuade — which is why she needs a thousand.
4. **The scholar, not the beauty.** Burton: she had read the books, annals and legends of earlier kings and collected a thousand books of histories; she knew the poets by heart. Her weapon is a library.
5. **The listener is paid to listen.** Sindbad overhears a poor porter (Hindbad in Lang; also named Sindbad in Burton) complaining in the street, invites him in, tells him one voyage a day, and pays him a hundred sequins each time. Reverse of Scheherazade: the teller pays the audience.
6. **The declared-dead man claims his own cargo.** Voyage 1: the "island" is a sleeping whale that dives when they light a fire; Sindbad is left behind and presumed drowned. Later, on a distant quay, he sees bales marked with his own name being sold "for the benefit of his family". The captain calls him a liar for claiming to be himself, until Sindbad proves it by telling his story and other merchants recognise him. *Identity restored by narrative and witnesses, not documents.*
7. **The reluctant courier.** Voyage 7 (Lang/Galland): the retired Sindbad has vowed never to sail again; the Caliph orders him to carry a letter and a gift to the King of Serendib. The gift includes a bed hung with cloth of gold, an agate vase showing a man aiming an arrow at a lion, and a table said to be Solomon's. On the way home pirates sell him as a slave; his master makes him shoot elephants with a bow; the elephants carry him to their burial hill of ivory so he will stop. Freed, he comes home; the Caliph has his stories written in gold letters and laid in the treasury.

## Images and objects
- A green "island" that trembles when a cooking fire is lit, then dives.
- Bales on a foreign quay with your own name on them.
- A porter's load carried home by a rich man's servants while the porter listens.
- An archer in a tree watching a herd of elephants close round it; the tree uprooted.
- A hill of elephant bones and tusks.
- Stories written in letters of gold and locked in a treasury.

## Character behaviours (in our words)
- Scheherazade: decisive, prepared, unshaken by her father's fear; she weeps on cue to get her sister brought in.
- Sindbad: a boastful old man who insists his wealth was earned by suffering; generous with the listener; addicted to the sea after swearing off it each time.
- The Caliph: kindly and immovable ("go you must; my honour demands it").
- Quotation (Lang, Sindbad): "I am that Sindbad whom you believe to be dead, and these are my possessions!"

## What retellings get wrong
- Scheherazade is not a lone seductress; she is a scholar with an accomplice, and the frame's violence is real.
- Sindbad is an old, rich storyteller recounting to a poor man; most adaptations make him young and present-tense.
- His last voyage in Galland/Lang is a diplomatic delivery job ending in elephant-hunting, not monsters. (And Idris, who claims to be Sindbad's heir, should know the hero ended as a courier with a bow.)

## Sensitivities
- Per canon: the threat Scheherazade lives under is never a joke; no harem imagery. Burton's notes and phrasing are often prurient and racist; do not borrow his framing.
- The frame's backstory involves adultery and execution of women; keep it off-screen.
